Overview

Most sites already have a downtime number, and most of those numbers are more optimistic than the floor would tell you if asked directly — not through dishonesty, but through two ordinary habits: a bad stop gets explained well enough, after the fact, to become "planned," and even a genuinely scheduled event only has a right to count as planned up to its standard time, so a 30-minute changeover that takes 40 is 30 minutes planned and 10 lost, never 40 planned just because it was expected. Both habits leave whoever owns the number quietly deciding after the fact what "planned" means.

This tool removes that discretion: a stop only counts as planned if it was scheduled in advance and only up to its agreed standard time, everything else is logged honestly as loss, and every cause is ranked not just by minutes but by occurrence count, because a single large breakdown and a hundred small stops adding up to the same total need genuinely different investigation — a sporadic, low-occurrence loss closes with a straightforward 5 Whys, a chronic, high-occurrence one needs a cause-and-effect session or it lands on one plausible answer while the loss barely moves. It deliberately covers one third of a bigger picture: Availability, not Performance (Schedule Adherence Tracker) or Quality (OTIF & Right-First-Time Dashboard) — a downtime number improving in isolation can hide a line now running slower or scrapping more to compensate.

Done properly, the top-ranked cause is classified as a prevention gap or a response gap and handed to PM Schedule Builder or Maintenance Response System by name, with a first response, an owner and a check-back date logged against it every review period — never left sitting at the top of the chart with nothing recorded.

At a glance
Time

Half a day to set your definitions and standard events register, then a couple of minutes at the point each stop ends.

Who's involved

Whoever's on the floor when a stop happens — operator, team leader, or you, if that's the same person — plus at least one person who can speak to what "normal" actually looks like for setting honest standards. For your own line, decide: who owns each standard time and is actually willing to challenge it if it's being missed every time; who checks a sample of logged entries against something real before the Pareto gets trusted; and who gets told first if a genuinely new pattern shows up.

What you'll need

The workbook, and your planned production schedule so you know the minutes you're measuring against. If the line already has sensor-based or MES-level monitoring, this guide is about extracting and mapping that data into the same model rather than replacing it — check specifically whether the existing system caps planned time at a standard or counts a scheduled event in full, the latter is the exact loophole this tool exists to close.

Before you start

Set your standard events register before logging starts, not after a bad week — it's much harder to agree an honest standard once a number exists that someone has a stake in defending. Start with your single most consequential line if time is short, not the whole site; this is a per-line tool by design, so one line isn't a compromise, it's the standard approach. If availability already gets reported upward, tell whoever receives that report before you touch the definition — tightening the rule will very likely make the number worse before it gets better.

If you're auditing an existing log rather than starting from nothing, check it for stops marked "planned" that either weren't genuinely scheduled in advance, or ran longer than standard with the overrun folded in anyway — expect to find both, that's normal drift, not a scandal.

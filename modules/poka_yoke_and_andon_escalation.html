Poka-Yoke & Andon Escalation

The most common mistakes become hard or impossible to make. When something still goes wrong, the right person knows within minutes, not at the end of the shift.

Overview

A defect that keeps recurring is not usually a training problem — it's a process that still allows the error to happen every time, regardless of how careful or experienced the person doing the work is. And a problem that only surfaces once it reaches the next process, or the customer, is not usually a detection problem either — it's the absence of a defined signal and a defined response at the moment the abnormality actually occurred.

This module has two connected but distinct parts. Mistake-proofing (poka-yoke) builds the process itself so a known, recurring error becomes physically difficult or impossible to make, using one of three methods: contact (stops the wrong part physically fitting), fixed-value (catches a missed or repeated action by counting), or motion-step (enforces the correct sequence). Andon is a separate, clear, fast escalation signal for the moment something abnormal happens anyway — what triggers a call, who responds, and what a completed response actually looks like, written down and visible at the workstation, not held in someone's head. It deliberately works from what the Non-Conformance Tracker (P3.1) or Customer Complaint system (P3.2) already say is recurring, not a brainstorm of everything that could theoretically go wrong, and most effective versions are physical or very simple digital — a locating pin, a jig, a counted-parts tray, a pull cord, a light — not a capital automation project.

Done well, the targeted defect rate drops and stays down, and every Andon call gets a completed response recorded against it, not just the ones that turned out to matter — with "responded" (someone physically checked the cause at the workstation inside the agreed time) kept strictly distinct from "resolved" (a second person has verified the underlying issue is genuinely fixed), since collapsing the two is one of the most common ways this quietly stops working.

At a glance
Time

A single poka-yoke and its paired Andon rule can usually be scoped, built and trialled inside one to two weeks for a genuinely simple, physical fix. Sensor-based or more involved mechanisms take longer.

Who's involved

The person who does the job, every time, without exception — a poka-yoke designed without them is a poka-yoke designed to be worked around. A supervisor or engineer to help translate the idea into something buildable. Whoever owns the area's Andon response, since they need to agree the response time before it goes live, not discover it afterwards.

For your own site, decide: who decides which defect gets the first poka-yoke (usually the area's supervisor or engineer, working from the ranked defect list); who can change the Andon response time once it's set; who gets told when a new poka-yoke goes live, and how far in advance; and who needs to agree before an Andon call gets closed as resolved — someone other than the person who responded, at minimum.

What you'll need

Whatever the specific mechanism needs, which varies — a locating pin or jig is a workshop job, a light-and-cord Andon signal is inexpensive and off-the-shelf. Keep the first build cheap and local; prove the mechanism before spending on anything more permanent. One recurring defect already ranked from the Non-Conformance Tracker (P3.1) or Customer Complaint log (P3.2).

Before you start

Start from one confirmed defect, already ranked, not a brainstorm of everything that could theoretically go wrong. Involve the people who do the job in the actual design conversation, at the workstation, not in a meeting room reviewing a drawing after the fact. Agree upfront, explicitly, that the first attempt will be simple and local — a team that starts with a capital-project mindset rarely ships anything inside the first month, and the defect keeps recurring while they wait.

Know before you start which of the two paths applies: is the recurring problem preventable at source (a physical constraint could stop the wrong action happening — build the poka-yoke), or not reasonably preventable at cost (the correct outcome depends on trained judgement a fixture cannot replicate — focus on the Andon side instead)?

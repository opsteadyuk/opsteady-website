Autonomous Maintenance Starter Pack

Operators catching the early signs before they become a breakdown, not engineering carrying all of it.

Overview

Most sites treat equipment care as maintenance's job, full stop — the operator runs the machine, and if something's wrong, they call it in and wait. But a maintenance team, however good, can't be standing at every asset all day, every day, and the operator already is. A noise that's slightly different, a drift in temperature, a fastener working loose, these are the earliest signs of a problem, and without a defined, simple checklist and a clear line between "normal" and "needs attention," operators have no structured way to act on what they're already noticing — it goes unmentioned until it becomes a stop.

This module gives operators a small number of genuinely doable checks (3 to 5 per asset), a clear, specific definition of normal versus abnormal for each, and a direct route for reporting when something isn't — piloted on one asset, chosen because Downtime Tracking's own Pareto (P5.1) already flagged it as high-value, not the easiest one to start with. It sits underneath PM Schedule Builder (P5.2) and Maintenance Response System (P5.3) as a standing practice, not a sequenced step reached by branching off either, and its findings feed back into Downtime Tracking's own Pareto over time as operators start catching issues before they become stops.

Done properly, checks happen daily without anyone prompting them, and at least one genuine early catch is logged — a real "not normal" flagged before it became a stop, not just ticks in a box every shift.

At a glance
Time

Half a day to define checks for the pilot asset and brief the first operator, once the asset and its basic failure modes are understood.

Who's involved

The operator who'll run the pilot, since their buy-in decides whether this becomes routine or drops off. Whoever owns maintenance for that asset, since they need to agree what "normal" actually means and commit to acting on flagged findings quickly. Whoever owns Downtime Tracking's Pareto, since the pilot asset should come from there.

For your own site, decide: who's the pilot operator, and have they agreed to it, not just been assigned it; who commits to acting on a flagged finding within a stated, short timeframe; and who owns expanding to further assets once the pilot is genuinely holding.

What you'll need

One asset to pilot on, ideally one already flagged as high-value from Downtime Tracking's Pareto. A basic understanding of that asset's common early failure signs, from whoever maintains it. The working file, P5.4_Autonomous_Maintenance_Starter_Pack_Workbook.xlsx, five tabs: How to use, Checklist (reusing Skills Matrix's capability-level layout), Sign-off log, Training sign-off, and a removable Example.

Before you start

Check first whether any informal basic care already happens, even without a name for it: if so, capture what an experienced operator already does by instinct and check it against the asset's real failure modes rather than assuming instinct alone covers everything worth catching; if not, build fresh, starting with the pilot asset from Downtime Tracking's Pareto. Start with one asset and one operator, not a site-wide rollout — enthusiasm for a broad launch is exactly what tends to collapse within a month. Tell maintenance, since flagged findings need a fast, visible response or the whole practice loses credibility within weeks, and tell whoever owns Downtime Tracking, since this is the feedback loop that should start shrinking its preventable-breakdown category over time.

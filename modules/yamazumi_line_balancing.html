Yamazumi / Line Balancing

Work spread evenly across every station, so the line runs close to its target rate instead of being paced by whichever station happens to be overloaded.

Overview

A line can be running flat out and still be delivering below takt, because busy is not the same as balanced. Overburden and waiting both hide inside individual workloads until someone actually observes and measures each station separately — a supervisor watching the line as a whole sees people working, which reads as productive, without seeing that one specific station is structurally unable to keep pace no matter how hard that person works, while another has slack nobody has named or used.

This module builds a yamazumi chart — a stacked bar chart showing each station's real, observed work content against takt, the target rate the line needs to run at — so the overloaded stations and the under-loaded ones are both visible at a glance instead of staying invisible inside a line that just looks generally busy. It is not a full industrial-engineering time-study system and needs no stopwatch specialist: a team builds it themselves from direct observation, then rebalances using three practical moves — splitting, combining, or shifting work elements between stations.

Done properly, at least one rebalancing action has actually been completed and trialled across several real cycles on the floor, not just proposed on paper, and the job instruction (P1.7) is updated to reflect the new distribution so the change survives past the person who designed it.

At a glance
Time

Observing and capturing real work content for a single line or cell, then building the first chart, usually takes a day or two of direct floor time. Trialling a rebalanced sequence before locking it as the new standard typically needs several full production cycles, not just a single walkthrough.

Who's involved

Whoever runs the line or cell being charted, since they know the real work content far better than any engineered standard sitting in a document. Someone who has already calculated takt or a clear target rate for this line, since the chart has no reference line without it.

For your own site, decide: who observes and times the work content, trained to do this accurately without disrupting the line's normal pace; who decides which rebalancing moves to trial, usually the area owner working directly with the people who run each station; who signs off moving a rebalanced sequence from trial to locked standard, confirming it has genuinely held across several real cycles; and who owns re-charting when mix, method, or staffing changes, named in advance so a chart doesn't quietly go stale.

What you'll need

A stopwatch or timer, and a simple template, paper or a basic spreadsheet, capable of stacking observed times into a bar per station — no specialist time-study software is required. A confirmed takt or target rate for the line, from Heijunka & Takt Time (P4.8), since the chart has no reference line without it. The working file, P4.9_Yamazumi_Chart_and_Rebalance_Log.xlsx, two tabs: a Yamazumi chart (one row per work element, stacked by station against a takt value you set once, naming which stations sit above takt and which have genuine spare capacity) and a Before and after log (one row per rebalance, tracking the highest station load before and after, whether the job instruction was updated, and whether it was trialled across several real cycles).

Before you start

Choose one line or cell to chart, not a site-wide rebalancing exercise from the outset. Confirm takt or the target rate is already known before starting. Agree upfront that the observation is of real, current work content, not the engineered ideal written into an old standard that may no longer reflect what actually happens — a chart built from a written job instruction rather than direct observation is one of the most common ways this module fails.

Work out first whether the line runs a single dominant product with work content that barely varies, or a genuinely mixed range where work content differs meaningfully between variants — a single static balance will fit some variants and badly misfit others in the second case, and needs the flexible work-sharing, floating-operator, or dominant-runner patterns instead of the standard build.

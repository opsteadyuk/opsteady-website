Ergonomics & Workplace Design

Work the body can actually sustain, not just survive: one station, five simple factors, one physical change confirmed by the people who do the job.

Overview

Most workstations were designed around the machine, the tooling, or the cycle time, not around the person working at it shift after shift for years — the physical experience of the operator rarely shows up as a defect on a quality report the way a dimensional issue would, so it shows up instead as things easy to blame on other causes: strain that builds slowly, quality that drifts worse toward the end of a shift, absence logged as a general ache, or resistance to a standard work instruction that's genuinely punishing to perform for eight hours.

This is a shop-floor method, not a clinical or occupational-health programme — it doesn't replace a formal DSE assessment or a qualified occupational health referral where one's needed. It observes one task against five factors (reach, posture, force, repetition, visibility), ranks the worst two or three, and makes one physical change — height, orientation, an assist device, a layout change — rather than asking the person to work around a bad design, since a design problem solved by asking people to be more careful will not hold under production pressure.

Done properly, the fix gets confirmed by the people doing the job, not just assumed to have worked, and then written into the job instruction (P1.7) and visual standard (P6.2) so it doesn't quietly drift back once the person who made the change moves on.

At a glance
Time

One full observation and improvement cycle typically takes half a day to a day, spread across watching the work at different points in a shift and making the physical change itself.

Who's involved

Whoever's doing the observation, and — critically — the people who actually do the job, both for the observation itself and for confirming afterward whether the change genuinely helped; ideally both a manager and the operators observe, since someone with a bit of distance notices things the person doing it daily has stopped seeing, while the operator knows exactly what feels hardest before any formal observation confirms it.

For your own site, decide: who chooses which station gets looked at first, and what signal they're using; who has authority and budget to make a small physical change without a lengthy approval process; who confirms afterward whether the change actually helped; and where a finding that needs a formal occupational health or DSE assessment gets escalated to.

What you'll need

The ergonomics observation sheet (P1.9_Ergonomics_Observation_and_Log.xlsx, two tabs), and whatever's needed to make the physical change itself — often simple (a stand, a tilted bin, a repositioned tool) rather than a capital project.

Before you start

Choose one task or station that already shows a real signal: reported strain, an absence pattern, quality that drifts worse later in a shift, or visible resistance to a standard that looks fine on paper. Observe the actual work being performed, not the intended layout or process document, at more than one point in the shift if fatigue is part of the concern, since a station can look fine in the first hour and clearly strained by the last.

Know before you start where the line sits between this module and a formal referral: an observed design fault (a reach, an awkward posture, a repeated motion) is this module's territory; a reported symptom (pain, numbness, a specific physical complaint) needs a direct, prompt referral to whoever owns occupational health on your site instead.

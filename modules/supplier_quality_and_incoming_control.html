Supplier Quality & Incoming Control

Supplier-caused defects caught or prevented at the gate, not discovered on the line or by the customer — and the same supplier problem stops quietly recurring month after month.

Overview

Most sites without this module treat incoming material as good until proven otherwise, usually proven by a line stop, a rework bill, or a customer complaint. That's not a policy anyone chose deliberately — it's the default state of a boundary nobody has explicitly claimed ownership of: production assumes goods-in checked it, goods-in assumes the supplier's certificate is enough, and the supplier assumes the last delivery, which nobody complained about, was fine. A supplier defect that reaches the line looks, from the floor, exactly like an internal process problem, and without a system that specifically tags the cause as supplier-related, it gets absorbed into general downtime or scrap figures, invisible as a pattern, while the supplier never learns their process has drifted.

This module has two connected parts: defining what "good" looks like on arrival for the items that actually matter, with the lightest verification method that genuinely confirms it — full inspection, a certificate accepted on trust, skip-lot sampling, or dock-to-stock where the risk genuinely doesn't justify a check — and logging supplier-caused defects using the same categories already used for internal non-conformances, so the two roll into a single Pareto rather than living in separate systems nobody cross-references. It isn't a full supplier-development or procurement system and doesn't require a formal audit programme to start — it gives a site a practical way to decide what to check, check only that, log what gets missed, and feed it back to the one place that can actually stop it recurring: the supplier's own process.

Done properly, critical incoming items have an agreed verification method, supplier defects are visible in the same review as internal non-conformances rather than siloed, and at least one repeating supplier issue has a documented response — either containment plus a permanent fix from the supplier, or a formal escalation if they haven't responded.

At a glance
Time

Ranking incoming items and defining verification methods for the top three or four usually takes a few days of focused work. Building the full supplier defect log and feedback path takes closer to a month of genuine use before it's reliable.

Who's involved

Whoever currently books in or inspects incoming goods, since they know which items actually cause problems in practice, not just on paper. Someone with authority to raise an issue with a supplier formally — procurement, quality, or the plant manager directly on a smaller site.

For your own site, decide: who decides which incoming items are critical enough to verify (usually quality or the plant manager, working from actual stop and rework history, not a supplier's reputation alone); who can change a verification method once it's set, and only after enough clean deliveries justify it; who raises a repeat supplier issue formally, named in advance; and who decides when an unresolved issue escalates beyond a normal conversation to a contractual or sourcing-level response.

What you'll need

A simple critical-to-quality list and verification log, on paper or a basic spreadsheet — no new inspection equipment is required to start; the first pass uses what the site already has. The working file, P3.5_Incoming_List_and_Supplier_Defect_Log.xlsx, two tabs: an Incoming list (one row per item, working out risk from actual stop/rework/customer-issue and safety/regulatory history, checking the chosen verification method against that risk in both directions) and a Supplier defect log (one row per defect, rolling into the same Pareto categories as internal non-conformances, with an escalation trigger you set — occurrences and a time window — defaulting to three in 90 days if left blank).

Before you start

Start from materials or suppliers that have actually caused a stop, rework, or a customer issue in recent months, not a theoretical review of the entire supplier base. Involve whoever currently does the goods-in check in defining what to verify, since they'll spot practical risks a desk review misses. Agree upfront that not everything gets full inspection — the whole discipline of this module is choosing the minimum that genuinely catches the risk.

If your suppliers are under a formal PPAP, ISIR, or equivalent certification requirement already, align this module's verification method with that existing documentation rather than duplicating it — a formal part-approval requirement is usually a point-in-time certification at launch, and this module's ongoing verification and feedback loop is what covers every delivery after that.

Overview

A launch date, once set, exerts a gravitational pull on every decision around it. Readiness gaps get reframed as manageable — "we'll sort that in week one" — because acknowledging a gap honestly would mean confronting the date. Most launches without this module follow the same pattern: the target date was fixed early, often before anyone fully understood what readiness would actually require, standards exist in draft, training has been rushed, nobody has confirmed the line can actually hit the required rate, and the site launches anyway because stopping feels harder than pushing through. What follows is weeks or months of firefighting — quality escapes nobody caught because the control wasn't proven, operators improvising because the standard was never finished, a maintenance team learning a new asset's failure modes in real time instead of in advance.

This module gives the site something to point to that isn't the date: a single readiness view across six standard categories — standards, skills, quality controls, capacity and rate, material readiness, and asset care — with every gap named, owned and either closed or explicitly accepted before volume starts. It doesn't build a parallel corporate stage-gate system, and it doesn't replace a formal automotive-style approval process where one already exists; it pulls together modules the site may already have and asks one question of each: is this actually ready for this specific launch.

Done properly, the go or no-go decision comes from that view, not the calendar, every critical gap was closed or visibly accepted by the person who owns that call, and the first period of volume ran without the usual scramble to fix missing standards, untrained people or unproven controls.

At a glance
Time

A short setup to agree categories and build the view, then a fixed rhythm of brief readiness reviews from the point the launch is confirmed until the process is running stably — 10 to 15 minutes is usually enough per review once the view exists.

Who's involved

Whoever owns the launch overall, plus a representative from each readiness category the launch actually touches — quality, engineering or maintenance, training, materials or planning.

For your own site, decide: who owns the overall readiness decision, usually the person accountable for the launch, since the go or no-go call needs one clear owner; who owns each readiness category, named individually so every gap has someone accountable for closing it; who facilitates the readiness reviews and presses for an honest status rather than letting "on track" go unchallenged; and who decides whether a gap is significant enough to delay, agreed in advance rather than negotiated fresh under pressure in the final week.

What you'll need

A simple checklist or board, physical or digital, whichever the site will actually keep current — no specialist software is required. The working file, P7.7_Launch_Readiness_and_Go_No_Go.xlsx, two tabs: a Readiness checklist (one row per category, the standard six ship with it, holding the owner, status, the evidence behind that status, a target date for each open gap, and whether the gap is critical for launch) and a Go or no-go record (the dated decision, what it was based on, any gap accepted rather than closed, and what actually happened as a result — critical-gap count at the moment of decision is copied in by hand deliberately, so a Go taken with gaps open can't quietly recalculate itself clean once those gaps later close).

Before you start

Build the readiness view for one real, upcoming launch, not a generic template applied retrospectively to something already running. Confirm with whoever owns the launch date that a no-go, or a short delay, is a genuinely available outcome before the first review happens, not a theoretical option nobody actually believes in. If your site has never run a formal readiness process before, treat the shift as cultural as much as procedural: a status of "not ready" needs to be a safe, normal thing to report, not something that reflects badly on whoever reports it.

Work out first whether this is a genuinely new product or process, or a significant change to something that already exists and runs: a new product or process should expect most or all six categories to need genuine, ground-up readiness work; a significant change to an existing process can often focus the readiness view specifically on what the change alters, rather than re-verifying everything from scratch.

Overview

Most sites have plenty of visual material on the floor — performance boards, signage, charts — but standing in front of any of it, you often can't tell whether things are alright right now. That's the stranger test: could someone who has never seen the site walk the floor and understand, without asking anyone, what should be happening, what is happening, and what's being done about the gap between them. Visual material accumulates for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it's useful — a board goes up because a system already produces the number, a sign goes up because an auditor once asked for one — and it can look like visual management from a distance while failing the stranger test up close.

This module builds a small Visual Controls Register of genuinely critical points, each one built so it shows three things together — the standard, the actual, and the gap and response — since a control missing any one of those is a rule, a report, or decoration, never a working control. Controls are built in cheap, changeable materials first — floor tape, laminated cards on Velcro or magnets, a whiteboard — rather than paint or engraving, so a standard that turns out wrong can be corrected in minutes rather than staying wrong because fixing it is expensive. Each one is also placed on a four-level power ladder — Indicator, Signal, Control, Guarantee — with "Good" (Control) as the standard aim for a genuinely critical point.

The first week produces one control, built properly and stranger-tested for real, rather than a site-wide push. After that, the register only keeps working if the stranger test becomes a rhythm rather than a one-off exercise at launch — the module's own failure library names a test run once at launch and never repeated as one of the most common ways this quietly stops working.

At a glance
Time

About 1 hour to run the stranger test on a single area properly, using the walk sheet. Building one control from a defined critical point to a working Good-level control typically takes about half a day, including time to define normal precisely and source changeable materials. After that, holding a control costs a few minutes a week — checking the gap column, updating the card when normal changes.

Who's involved

One named person to own the register. Someone to build the first control — ideally the person who works at that point daily, since they know what normal actually looks like. A genuine stranger, or someone deliberately trying to see the site as one, to run the test.

Before starting, decide for your own site: who owns updating a control when the process it describes changes; who is told when a control's standard changes; who can add a new critical point to the register; who runs the periodic stranger test, and how often; and who is the named person who responds to a gap at that specific control. On a multi-shift site, that last question needs a separate named answer per shift, not one name assumed to cover all of them.

What you'll need

The Visual Controls Register (Excel) and the stranger-test walk sheet (Word), both supplied with this module. Cheap, changeable materials — floor tape, laminated cards, Velcro or magnetic strips, a whiteboard marker — not paint or engraving.

Before you start

A critical point earns a place on the register if the honest answer to any one of three questions is yes: would drift here go unnoticed for more than a shift if nothing were visible; does noticing it late cost something real — safety, quality or output; and can "normal" be defined clearly enough that a deviation is obvious at a glance. Leave off anything already caught by an alarm, an interlock or automated monitoring — this module is for the things nobody is watching, not the things a machine already is.

Run the stranger test before building anything — it tells you where to spend the half day, rather than guessing which area looks worst — and pick the highest-risk critical point first, not the easiest one to build.

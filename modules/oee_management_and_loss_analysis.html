OEE Management & Loss Analysis

The biggest equipment losses visible by type and size, so improvement effort goes to the real constraint on output, not whichever stop was loudest or most recent.

Overview

Most sites without this module know whether an asset is "broken or not," from stop data alone, and stop there — a view that misses two entire categories of loss a simple stop log never shows: running slower than it should, and producing units that don't count as good output even while the machine is technically running. Before OEE means anything, three genuinely ambiguous boundary questions have to be answered explicitly: what counts as Planned Production Time, what's the ideal or designed cycle time being measured against, and what exactly counts as a good unit, and at what point in the process — different answers to any one can move the resulting number substantially without real performance having changed at all.

This module teaches how to calculate Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) — Availability multiplied by Performance multiplied by Quality, deliberately multiplying rather than adding, so a genuinely weak factor drags the whole number down instead of being averaged away by two strong ones — and how to structure those losses into Seiichi Nakajima's Six Big Losses view, ranked by real impact rather than by whichever loss feels most memorable. It builds directly on downtime data Downtime Tracking & Pareto Pack (P5.1) may already be capturing for Availability, and extends it to cover the two factors a stop log alone is blind to.

Done properly, the top-ranked loss is handed to whoever actually owns fixing it — planned maintenance, autonomous care, or process improvement — with a genuine, tracked action against it, not left as a number sitting in a report.

At a glance
Time

Calculating a first OEE figure for one asset, once basic downtime data exists, typically takes a day or two. Capturing Performance and Quality losses properly, if they aren't already tracked, takes longer, often a couple of weeks of deliberate observation to build a reliable baseline.

Who's involved

Whoever owns the asset's downtime data already, usually maintenance or the area supervisor. Someone from quality who can supply or help define the Quality factor's data. Whoever will actually own acting on the top loss once it's ranked.

For your own site, decide: who decides the operating time definition, and who can change it later, usually the plant manager or a cross-functional group, since this single decision affects every OEE number that follows; who owns capturing Performance loss data if it isn't already tracked; who owns the Quality factor's definition, working with the area rather than decided unilaterally; and who receives the top-ranked loss once identified, named per loss category in advance.

What you'll need

A simple OEE calculation sheet and loss tree, on paper or a basic spreadsheet — no new equipment or automated data collection is required to start. Existing downtime data from Downtime Tracking & Pareto Pack (P5.1) as the starting point for Availability. The working file, P5.5_OEE_Loss_Analysis_Pack.xlsx, three tabs: OEE calculation (all four figures calculated, not typed, refusing arithmetic that can't be true, and flagging any move of more than five points with no recorded cause), Loss tree (the Six Big Losses ranked by the sheet on real time or output lost, flagging a top loss with no named owner or action), and Top loss action (reads the top-ranked loss and works out which module owns it).

Before you start

Choose one critical asset or line, ideally the site's actual constraint, not the easiest asset to demonstrate the tool on. Confirm basic downtime data is already being captured before starting, since Availability is the one factor most sites already have some data for. Agree the operating time definition explicitly before calculating anything: which shifts count, which planned stops are excluded, and how breaks are treated.

Work out first whether you're building this OEE definition from nothing, or auditing an existing figure already in use: building from nothing means working through each boundary definition deliberately, since nothing has been decided yet; auditing an existing figure means checking it against the same three boundary questions before accepting it, since a generous or inconsistent definition can look better than the real performance it's meant to represent.

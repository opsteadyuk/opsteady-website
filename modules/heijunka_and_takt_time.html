Heijunka & Takt Time

The site runs to a stable, visible rhythm set by real customer demand, instead of swinging between overtime peaks and empty troughs. Everyone can see what pace the day needs to run at, and the plan itself stops being the source of the chaos.

Overview

Most sites without this module run a schedule that reacts to whatever's loudest: a big order arrives, everything else gets pushed aside, overtime gets booked to catch up, and the following week there's a lull because the surge pulled work forward that would otherwise have arrived steadily.

The lumps rarely start in planning. They start upstream: sales accepting whatever date the customer asks for, a month end shipping push, MRP releasing work in weekly batches, one large customer ordering a month's worth in a single drop. Planning inherits the lumps and does its best with them. Read this as a scheduling fix and it will fail, because the schedule is where the problem shows up, not where it comes from.

This module teaches two connected ideas that sit between capacity planning and the daily schedule. Takt time is a pace: take the time you actually have to produce, divide it by what customers actually order, and you get the rate one unit needs to be finished at to keep up. No faster, no slower. Heijunka is how you hold that pace: instead of chasing whatever order feels most urgent this hour, you spread the work, the volume and where it matters the mix, evenly across a chosen horizon (day, week, or a smaller repeating unit called a pitch), so every day looks like the last one.

A schedule that chases orders creates overburden on the days demand spikes and idle capacity on the days it doesn't, and both are expensive in ways that don't show up on a single KPI. Overburden pushes a team into error-prone overtime and skipped standards. Idle time hides that a process still has spare capacity to give. Together they make a real problem far harder to see, because normal variation in workload masks the difference between "busy because demand spiked" and "busy because something's actually wrong". A level schedule removes that noise.

It has to be calculated against real available capacity and the site's true constraint, ideally already confirmed by Capacity Planning Model (P4.3) and Bottleneck Analysis Tool (P4.1). Levelling against a guessed capacity or the wrong constraint just moves the chaos somewhere else.

At a glance
Time

A few days to calculate takt and build a first levelled plan for one product family, once real demand data is in hand. Trialling and adjusting the levelled sequence usually needs one to two full horizons, weeks or pitches, before it's ready to lock as the standard.

Who's involved

Whoever holds real demand data, usually planning or a customer-facing function, since a takt calculated from an estimate rather than genuine order history will mislead everyone who relies on it. Whoever schedules the floor day to day, since they'll be the one actually running the levelled sequence once it's built. And whoever owns the customer promise, sales or customer service, because a level plan changes what the site can say yes to, and they find that out first.

For your own site, decide: who decides the horizon to level against (day, week, or pitch), usually planning working with the area owner; who can change takt once it's calculated, and only when demand or capacity genuinely changes, not on a hunch; who owns keeping the heijunka board updated and followed shift to shift; and who signs off moving from trial to locked standard, confirming the levelled plan has genuinely held for at least one full horizon.

What you'll need

A simple takt calculation sheet and a levelled plan, on paper or a basic spreadsheet. No specialist software or finite-scheduling system is required to start. A heijunka device, physical or a simple visual board: a slotted card holder or a marked sequence board is standard and inexpensive.

The working file, P4.8_Heijunka_Takt_Time_Pack.xlsx, three tabs: Takt calculation (calculated, not typed, flagging where the demand figure came from an estimate rather than genuine order history), Levelled plan (quantity per slot, mix share, and run sequence worked out for you), and Heijunka board (what the floor actually follows, distinguishing expediting from a process that genuinely couldn't follow the plan).

Before you start

Confirm capacity and the site's real constraint are already understood, ideally from Capacity Planning Model (P4.3) and Bottleneck Analysis Tool (P4.1), before calculating takt. Know the changeover time between the variants you intend to sequence; if nobody can tell you, time three before you start, because changeover is what decides how tight the mix sequence can be. Agree the horizon to be levelled, day, week, or pitch, before starting, since this decision shapes everything that follows.

Understand before you start that takt is not a target to beat. Running faster than takt doesn't help, it just builds inventory the customer hasn't asked for yet. The goal is to run steadily at that pace, not to race it.

Work out first whether demand for this product family is relatively stable across the chosen horizon, or significantly variable. Stable means weekly totals stay within about a fifth of the average most weeks, and takes a standard levelled volume pattern spread evenly across the horizon. More variable than that needs a shorter pitch, a frozen horizon, or volume-only levelling instead of a single fixed takt that will feel wrong at both ends.

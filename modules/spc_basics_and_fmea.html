SPC Basics & FMEA

Critical processes watched for early signs of drift, not just checked for defects after the fact — and the highest-risk failure modes thought through before they happen, not discovered by the customer.

Overview

Most quality activity on a site is reactive by default: something scraps, a complaint arrives, an inspection fails, and only then does anyone look closely at the process behind it — which only catches what's already gone wrong at least once, usually more than once, before anyone noticed a pattern. A process can run for weeks quietly drifting toward a limit nobody's tracking, and a new or changed process carries failure modes nobody's experienced yet, because it hasn't run long enough to fail in every way it eventually will.

This module teaches two connected, deliberately practical tools, not a full statistical or Six Sigma curriculum. Statistical process control (SPC) plots a critical measurement over time so ordinary variation and a genuine problem can be told apart before the problem produces a defect, with control limits calculated from the process's own real behaviour rather than the specification tolerance. Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) is a structured way of thinking through what could go wrong before a process runs, scored on severity, occurrence and detection and combined into an Opsteady FMEA Priority rating rather than a single multiplied number, since a multiplied score can rank two very different real risks identically.

Done properly, one chart is being watched and genuinely reacted to without anyone needing to prompt it, and one FMEA's highest-priority actions carry a named owner and a due date rather than sitting as a completed scoring exercise in a folder.

At a glance
Time

Setting a first control chart up — choosing the measure and collecting enough points to set preliminary limits — usually takes one to two weeks of the process running normally. A first-pass process FMEA for a single process typically takes half a day to a full day of focused time with the right people in the room, not spread thinly across weeks.

Who's involved

For SPC: whoever already collects or can easily collect the measurement, and whoever owns the reaction when a point goes out of limits. For FMEA: the people who actually run the process, plus anyone with technical or engineering knowledge of how it can fail — a room without the operator misses failure modes only they would know about.

For your own site, decide: who decides which process gets the first control chart (usually the quality lead or supervisor, working from ranked risk and defect data); who can change a control limit once it's set, and only once enough new stable data justifies it; who owns the reaction when a chart signals a problem, named in advance, not decided in the moment; and who signs off the FMEA's action list versus who checks the actions actually happened — two different people, so completion isn't self-certified.

What you'll need

A simple chart, on paper or a basic spreadsheet, is enough to start — no specialist SPC software is required for a single critical process. A blank process FMEA template, structured by process step. The working file, P3.4_Control_Chart_and_FMEA.xlsx, three tabs: Control chart (limits calculated from the process's own behaviour, drawing no limits at all until at least 20 baseline points exist), Process FMEA (severity/occurrence/detection scored against a Rating scales tab, with Opsteady FMEA Priority calculated automatically), and Rating scales (the full 1–10 definitions).

Before you start

Choose the process from what the Non-Conformance Tracker (P3.1), the OTIF & RFT Dashboard (P4.5), or straightforward customer risk already says is critical or unstable — not from a guess at what might be interesting to chart. Confirm data is already being collected, or can be, without building new infrastructure first. For FMEA, hold the session with the people who run the process now, before the process changes, not as a retrospective exercise after something's already gone wrong.

Work out first whether the risk in front of you is an existing process drifting or a process that hasn't run long enough to show its failure modes yet: an existing, unstable or recurring-defect process starts with SPC; a new or significantly changed process not yet run at volume starts with FMEA; if both genuinely apply, run the FMEA first, since a process about to change should have its new failure modes considered before setting control limits the change will invalidate anyway.

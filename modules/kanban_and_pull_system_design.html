Kanban & Pull System Design

Material and work move only when the downstream process actually needs them, not because a schedule says to make more — one clear pull loop, running and trusted, replacing expediting and excess inventory with a signal everyone understands.

Overview

Most sites run on push logic by default, even when nobody calls it that: each process makes what its own schedule tells it to, independent of whether the next process downstream is actually ready for it. A push schedule is built from a forecast, and a forecast is wrong by the time it reaches the floor, always by some margin. The result is familiar everywhere it happens — inventory piles up between processes running at different speeds, and the moment demand shifts, someone is expediting a shortage in one place while another area quietly drowns in stock nobody asked for.

This module turns the capacity and inventory work already done elsewhere in the P4 pillar into a practical pull system — a defined loop where material or work only moves because the next process signalled it actually needs more. It's a physical or basic-digital kanban loop, a card, an empty container, a supermarket location, or a FIFO lane, that a team can run and adjust itself, without a project team or new software. The starting quantity is calculated from real usage and real replenishment time for that specific connection, not a round number, and the loop isn't considered designed until an explicit reaction rule exists for what happens if a signal is raised and nobody responds.

Done well, one clear pull loop is genuinely running: the downstream process is no longer stopped by missing material from it, excess inventory has genuinely fallen rather than just moved somewhere else, and the team manages the signal day to day without needing constant supervision.

At a glance
Time

Mapping current push points and designing one loop usually takes a few days — most of that time goes on calculating a defensible starting quantity from real usage data rather than guessing. Trialling and adjusting the loop before locking it as the standard typically takes one to two weeks of real running.

Who's involved

Whoever runs both ends of the connection, upstream and downstream, since they know the real rhythm of demand and replenishment better than a spreadsheet does. Someone who understands the site's actual constraint, ideally already identified through Bottleneck Analysis Tool (P4.1), since a pull loop that ignores the real constraint will either starve it or feed it more than it can process.

For your own site, decide: who decides the pilot connection, usually whoever owns the area, working from where inventory or expediting pain is actually worst; who can change a kanban quantity once it's set, and only after enough real running data justifies it; who owns the reaction when a signal goes unanswered, named in advance and tied into the same escalation path as any other abnormality; and who signs off moving from pilot to locked standard.

What you'll need

Cards, containers, or floor markings for a physical loop — inexpensive and usually already available on site. No specialist software is required to start; a basic-digital equivalent is a later refinement, not a prerequisite.

Before you start

Choose one product family or process connection to pilot, not the whole value stream at once. Confirm the constraint and the real flow are already understood, ideally from Bottleneck Analysis Tool (P4.1) or a Value Stream Map (P7.1), before designing the loop around them. Agree explicitly that the first loop will be simple and local, not a site-wide rollout planned before a single loop has been proven.

Work out first whether demand for this item is relatively stable and repeating, or varies significantly and unpredictably: stable, repeating demand takes the standard fixed-quantity kanban design; highly variable or unpredictable demand needs a signal kanban or sequenced pull instead, since a fixed-quantity kanban will swing wildly under it.

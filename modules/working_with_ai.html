Working with AI

Know where AI helps on your site, and where it will quietly mislead you.

Overview

Most sites end up in one of two places with AI, and both waste something real. Some avoid it entirely — a real time saving sits unused because nobody drew a line between what's safe to try and what isn't, so it feels safer to try none of it. Others go the other way: AI gets asked not just to sort and summarise but to decide, and its answer gets treated as a finding rather than a first draft, one shortcut at a time, because the tool sounds exactly as confident when it's wrong as when it's right.

This module draws that line on your own site, in your own words, and gives you a habit that keeps you on the right side of it. AI output reads as confident and well-formed whether it's accurate or not — there's no visual difference between a correct summary and a wrong one — so a deliberate sanity-check, every time, does the job instinct can't. The core distinction is seeing versus deciding: sorting, summarising and first-pass categorising are safe to hand to AI and check; deciding what a finding means and what to do about it stays a human call, every time.

Done properly, you can name, in your own words, one task AI should never be trusted to decide on your site — and the habit spreads because one person tried a genuinely tedious task, checked it, and told someone else what they found, not because of a rollout or a designated AI person.

At a glance
Time

No fixed setup. Trying a first task takes 10 to 20 minutes, most of it the sanity check, not the AI step itself.

Who's involved

Whoever already owns the tedious task — not a designated AI lead, since creating that role tends to create a bottleneck where everyone waits for the one person instead of learning the habit themselves.

For your own site, decide: who decides an AI tool is approved for use at all; who decides which data categories are safe to paste in, and which aren't; who reviews the first output on a new task before anyone treats it as trustworthy; who gets told when AI is being used on something that feeds a number someone else reports upward; and who decides when a wrong-and-confident result is serious enough to stop and re-check the whole habit, not just that one task.

What you'll need

Access to a general-purpose AI tool — which one is a real decision for your site, not something to default to whatever's open in a browser tab, and depends on what your organisation already runs and what your data will tolerate leaving the building. Four routes exist, in order of preference: whatever your organisation already provides (the right answer for most sites most of the time, since it's inside a data agreement already signed); a general consumer tool on an individual account (fast, but you don't control where the text goes or how long it's kept — nothing personal, confidential, or proprietary should go in); an enterprise or self-hosted deployment (the right answer where the data you most want to sort is the data you least want to send anywhere); or none of them, for data that's personal, confidential, under an NDA or regulated — a legitimate outcome, not a gap to route around.

Before you start

Check your own company's policy first — this module's checklist is a starting position, not a replacement for whatever your organisation already has; if a policy exists, it decides this, not this guide. Pick one task that's data-heavy, low-judgement, and already sitting on someone's desk this week — not the hardest problem on site, the most tedious one. Check the data-privacy checklist on the working file before the first paste, not after, since data sent somewhere it shouldn't have gone can't be recalled.

Work out first whether the task is sorting, summarising or categorising existing data, or deciding what the data means or what to do next: sorting, summarising, categorising is safe to try, following the standard sanity check; deciding what it means stays a human call — AI can help lay out options, but the decision itself stays yours.

Energy & Environmental Waste

Energy and environmental performance stop being a separate "green" topic and become part of the same loss-and-cost conversation as scrap, downtime and overtime.

Overview

Most sites without this module treat energy and environmental performance as something separate — a utility bill that arrives once a month, or a "green" initiative run alongside, rather than inside, the everyday operational improvement system. The bill lands on someone's desk, gets paid, and nobody on the floor ever connects it to anything specific they could actually change. Meanwhile, the exact same kind of waste tracked obsessively elsewhere — a machine running when it doesn't need to, a leak nobody's fixed — goes almost entirely unmeasured here simply because it shows up on an invoice instead of a downtime log.

This module extends the same waste-and-cost lens the site already applies to scrap, downtime and overtime to energy, water, materials and environmental impact: compressed air leaks, idle equipment, excessive heating or cooling, water use, packaging or material waste. It builds a simple, ranked view of the main loss sources with rough cost estimates, named owners, and at least one live improvement already underway — naming and ranking specific, local loss sources turns an abstract monthly number into something with a specific cause and a specific owner. It's not a full environmental management system or an ISO 50001 implementation, and the boundary with HSE is explicit: HSE owns environmental compliance, permits and the statutory register; this module owns the everyday operational waste-and-cost view.

Done properly, the top-ranked losses each have a named owner, at least one has a measurable reduction linked to a specific action, and the topic sits inside the same review as other cost and waste priorities rather than off to the side in an annual sustainability report nobody in daily operations reads.

At a glance
Time

A walk of the main energy-using or waste-generating areas, typically an hour or two for a single area, plus access to existing utility bills or basic metering data to sanity-check estimates against.

Who's involved

Whoever owns operational improvement generally, plus anyone with visibility into utility costs — a site administrator, finance contact, or facilities lead — who can supply or confirm basic energy and water spend.

For your own site, decide: who leads the walk and builds the first-pass loss list, usually whoever leads other operational waste-spotting exercises; who supplies or confirms utility cost data, named specifically since it's often owned outside operations; who owns each ranked loss source individually; and who decides whether a loss is material enough to feed into the site's Cost Deployment or Cost-of-Quality view, agreed in advance rather than debated fresh for every item found.

What you'll need

Recent utility bills, even at the monthly total level, are enough to start — no specialist metering, audit software, or carbon-accounting tools are required for the standard version. The working file, P8.5_Loss_List_and_Cost_Link.xlsx, two tabs: a Loss list (one row per source — what it is, whether energy or material/environmental, where found, how estimated, rough cost, named owner, linked improvement method, and whether it's actually been started, ranked by cost with each source's share of the total) and a Cost deployment link tab (whether each source is material enough to escalate, and which view it was fed into, naming any material source that hasn't been escalated yet).

Before you start

Agree upfront that the first pass will be practical and local — walking the site and estimating roughly, not a full carbon footprint exercise. Choose one area to walk first if the whole site feels like too much for an initial pass.

Work out first whether the loss you're chasing is primarily energy-related or primarily material and environmental waste: energy-related sources (compressed air, idle equipment, heating and cooling) are usually found by walking areas when production is stopped or slow, listening and looking for what's still running; material or environmental waste (packaging, water use, scrap-adjacent material loss) is usually found by looking at what leaves the site as waste or excess material. If both genuinely matter, cover both in the same first pass rather than treating them as separate exercises.

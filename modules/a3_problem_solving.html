A3 Problem-Solving Templates

One page, root cause verified before a single countermeasure gets written — because that sequence, not the paper size, is the entire discipline.

Overview

An A3 is named after the sheet it fits on, roughly 11 by 17 inches, and the constraint is the entire point: if you cannot explain a problem on one sheet, you do not understand it well enough yet. But the paper size isn't what makes A3 thinking work, the sequence is — left side first (background, current condition, goal, root cause, verified with data or direct observation, never assumed), and only then the right side (countermeasures, a plan, and a way to check whether it worked). The single most common way A3 problem-solving fails, documented consistently across the field, is skipping straight to the right side: a countermeasure gets written first because someone already had an idea, and the "analysis" on the left gets built backward afterward to justify it.

The template is built as two columns specifically because the two sides do genuinely different work — the left column has nowhere on it to put a countermeasure, and the right column opens with a direct STOP question: is the root cause verified with data or direct observation, not assumed? Answering that honestly, before writing a single countermeasure, is the entire value of this tool. "Countermeasure," not "solution," is deliberate language too: a solution implies you're finished, a countermeasure is your best current thinking, being tested, with a real date to check whether it actually held — if it doesn't, that's the follow-up box doing exactly the job it exists to do, not a failed A3.

Done properly, root cause boxes get filled with something systemic — a fixture, a schedule, a standard — never "operator error" as the final answer, and follow-up dates actually get kept: an A3 that never gets checked against its goal isn't finished, it's just been abandoned with a nicer format.

At a glance
Time

Not a one-sitting exercise. Expect it to develop over days or weeks as data gets gathered and root cause gets genuinely verified.

Who's involved

A coach or manager to review it with — A3 thinking is meant to be a dialogue, not a solo form-filling exercise. If you're genuinely the most senior person likely to review it, a peer, another manager or team leader, even outside your own area, still works; what matters is a second person asking "how do you know" before you cross to the right side, not their seniority.

For your own site, decide: who's the coach or manager reviewing each A3 before it crosses to the right side; who owns the follow-up date, and who checks it actually gets kept; and who needs to be involved before countermeasures land on their desk unshaped.

What you'll need

The template, printed on A4 landscape — works on any standard printer; an A3 printer, if available, is a bonus, not a requirement. Two working files: P7.4_A3_Problem_Solving_Template.docx (the printable one-page template itself, seven boxes in sequence with a STOP checkpoint before box 5) and P7.4_A3_Register_Workbook.xlsx (two tabs — a Register logging every A3 in flight with a live days-open figure, and a Dashboard reading open/closed counts, average days open, and a stage breakdown straight from it).

Before you start

Pick one real, current problem, not a hypothetical. Work the left side first — background, current condition backed by real data, and a specific measurable goal, not "improve quality" — and work the root cause box properly using 5 Whys, a fishbone diagram, or a Pareto chart, verified against data or direct observation. Answer the STOP question honestly before writing a single countermeasure.

If you're working genuinely alone with no one to review it, ask yourself the same three questions a coach would before crossing to the right side: what data or observation actually proves this root cause, could you defend this to someone who'd push back, and does your last "why" name something systemic rather than a person.

A defined response when something breaks, not whoever's free and however long it takes.

Overview

When a breakdown happens, it isn't always the failure itself that costs the most time. Often it's what happens next: nobody's quite sure who to call first, the part that's needed is somewhere nobody can immediately locate, and there's no clear point at which the issue stops being handled informally and gets escalated to someone with more authority or resource. The breakdown might have been unavoidable. The hour spent figuring out how to respond to it usually wasn't.

This module maps the response path in advance: first call, second call, the exact point where it escalates (with a specific time attached, not "if it's taking a while"), and where the critical spares actually are. It's reached specifically when Downtime Tracking's own Pareto is dominated by slow or chaotic response time rather than breakdown frequency itself — a sibling branch, PM Schedule Builder & Compliance Tracker (P5.2), exists for sites whose real issue is PM being skipped instead. The mechanism targets Mean Time To Repair specifically, a distinct problem from Mean Time Between Failures: a site can have genuinely infrequent breakdowns and still lose enormous time to each one if the response itself is disorganised.

Done properly, response time visibly trends down in the log, the escalation trigger actually gets used when its time threshold passes rather than everyone quietly avoiding it, and the critical spares list stays checked rather than going stale as stock genuinely moves.

At a glance
Time

Half a day to map the response path for the single highest-impact asset, once real downtime data on response time, not just frequency, is in hand.

Who's involved

Whoever's actually called when something breaks, maintenance or otherwise. Whoever has authority to bring in outside support once the escalation trigger fires. Whoever owns Downtime Tracking's data, since response time needs to be visible there to confirm this is the right branch.

For your own site, decide: who is the confirmed first call, and do they know it; who has authority to bring in outside help once the escalation trigger fires; and who owns keeping the critical spares list and its locations current.

What you'll need

Downtime Tracking's data, specifically showing response time as the dominant issue, not breakdown count. Real detail from a recent breakdown, not a theoretical best case, since the actual delays are usually more informative than anyone expects.

Before you start

Map the real response path for a recent breakdown before designing the ideal one — who actually got called, in what order, and how long each step took, not the version that sounds efficient in hindsight. The gap between what actually happened and what should happen is where this module's value sits. Tell everyone who might be first on the scene when something breaks, since the structure only works if it's the thing people actually reach for, and tell whoever's named at the escalation point, since being named without being told is worse than not having an escalation point at all.

Check first whether breakdown severity varies meaningfully across your assets — if most breakdowns carry similar urgency, one response chain and one escalation trigger applies uniformly; if severity genuinely varies, priority-tiered response is the Pro variant instead.

What it takes

Setup timeAbout 1 hour to run the stranger test on a single area properly, using the walk sheet: walking it, noting what a stranger would and wouldn't understand. Building one control from a defined critical point to a working Good-level control typically takes about half a day, including the time to define normal precisely and source changeable materials. After that, holding a control costs a few minutes a week: checking the gap column, updating the card when normal changes.
Who's involvedSomeone to own the register, one name. Someone to build the first control, ideally the person who works at that point daily, since they know what normal actually looks like. A genuine stranger, or someone deliberately trying to see the site as one, to run the test.
MaterialsThe Visual Controls Register (Excel) and the stranger-test walk sheet (Word), both supplied with this module. Floor tape, laminated cards, Velcro or magnetic strips, a whiteboard marker: cheap, changeable materials, not paint or engraving. See section 2 for why that matters.

Common questions

How do I actually tell data from status, when it's not as obvious as the yesterday's-output-number example?
Ask two questions of whatever's on the wall. First, does it tell you what should be happening right now, not just what happened at some point in the past? Second, if the answer to the first question is yes, can you tell, without doing any mental arithmetic or asking anyone, whether the actual is inside or outside that standard? A board showing "Output: 4,200 units, week 32" fails both: there's no target next to it, and even if you happened to know the target was 4,500, you'd still be doing the comparison in your head, which means the board isn't really doing it for you. A board showing "Target: 4,500. Actual: 4,200. Gap: staffing short on lines 2 and 3, cover requested" passes both, because the comparison is already made and the reason is already there. The genuinely tricky cases are boards that show a trend line or a red-amber-green status without a stated target: they look like status because they're colour-coded, but if you can't say what the underlying number needed to be to earn that colour, it's still data wearing a status board's clothes. If you're unsure about a specific board on your site, ask whoever reads it daily what "alright" looks like on it, without letting them check. If they can't answer in a couple of seconds, it's failing the same test a stranger would fail.
What do we do when nothing on our site clearly meets the risk bar for a visual control?
That's a legitimate outcome, not a sign you're doing the exercise wrong. The three risk questions in section 4 (would drift go unnoticed for more than a shift, does noticing late cost something real, can normal be defined precisely enough) are deliberately a filter, not a target you're meant to fill with a set number of controls. A genuinely well-run, well-automated, low-risk area might honestly produce two or three critical points rather than a dozen. Resist the pull to lower the bar just to have something to show for the exercise; a control built for a point that didn't really need one is exactly the kind of visual noise section 9 warns about, and it dilutes the ones that actually matter. If it feels like nothing qualifies, the more useful move is usually to re-run the stranger test itself and ask what a genuinely new person would struggle to work out, rather than to soften the risk questions until something passes.
How does the four-level ladder relate to which materials I should actually use?
They're two separate decisions that happen to interact. The ladder (Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent) is about how much power the control has over behaviour, a sign versus a light versus a physical zone versus a mechanical stop. The materials principle (tape before paint, cards before engraving) is about how easy the control is to correct once it exists, regardless of which level it's built to. In practice, most Good-level controls are built from genuinely cheap, changeable materials at first, floor tape marking a zone, a laminated card on Velcro, precisely because you don't yet know if the standard you've defined is right. Once a control has held unchanged through several stranger tests and review cycles, upgrading the materials, and sometimes the level itself, from Good to something closer to Excellent, becomes worth considering. What you should never do is jump straight to a permanent, expensive version of a control before it's proven itself, whatever level you're aiming for; a beautifully engraved sign at the Poor level is still Poor, and a freshly painted line that turns out to be the wrong number is now an afternoon's grinding work to fix rather than a five-minute one.
What do we actually do about a gap column that nobody's filling in?
First, work out whether it's a naming problem or a caring problem, because the fix is different for each. If nobody's named as the person who responds, that's the fix: use the decide-who's-involved structure in section 4 and put an actual name against it, not "the shift" or "whoever's around." If someone is named and it's still not being filled in, the honest next step isn't a sterner reminder, it's asking that person directly why not. Common answers are that raising the gap has never actually led to anything happening, which is a genuine reason to stop bothering, or that filling it in feels like admitting to a problem in a way that's been punished before, which is a culture issue, not a visual management one. Either way, a gap column that's been blank for more than a review cycle is one of the clearest early warning signs in section 9's failure library, and it's worth treating as a signal that something upstream of the board needs attention before you touch the board itself.
How is this different from doing full 5S properly?
This module is deliberately the smallest slice of workplace organisation that earns the name, not a lighter version of 5S dressed up under a different title. Full 5S, and the deeper Advanced Visual Management set this module leads into, covers sorting, setting in order, shining, standardising and sustaining across a whole area or site, as a system. This module covers one narrower thing well: making a handful of genuinely critical points tell the truth about normal, actual and the gap between them, using the stranger test as the check. You can run this module without ever doing a formal 5S event, and plenty of sites should, since a handful of properly built controls beats a site-wide push that reverts within a couple of months, which is exactly the failure named first in section 9's library. Once a small number of controls are genuinely holding, that's the point where extending into full 5S or layered audits starts to make sense, not before.

Who We Are

There's no name or photograph on this site

Who's behind this

That's deliberate, and worth being straight about: Opsteady is a small operation, run by one person, with no team, no office and no sales function. That's a choice, not a stage it's trying to grow out of. Nobody will call you.

Which means you can't judge this by a CV, so judge it by the work. Take the Health Check. Read the report. If it doesn't sound like someone who has stood on a floor at six in the morning, you'll know within a page.

Where this comes from

Twenty years, four plants, different sectors, from apprentice to running the full site.

It starts in a plant where the next youngest person was twenty years older. There was no training framework and no plan, just a stated ambition that this group would run the place one day. Most of the workforce assumed we were there to replace them, so nobody would show us anything. We watched and didn't touch, for a long time, until enough trust was built for people to let us help, and to answer our endless how and why.

And what they taught wasn't written down anywhere. The critical process knowledge lived in individuals. Every operator had their own way of doing the job and every way produced a different result, so how the line ran depended entirely on which shift you were with. There were standards. There were SOPs. The floor looked excellent. Underneath it, none of that described how the place actually worked.

That gap, between the system on paper and the practice on the floor, is the most useful thing to look for in an operation. It's why the Health Check asks about both separately, and never averages them into one comfortable score.

Then a first improvement team: a mixed group, a week on a line, deep clean, repairs, the full 5S treatment. The principle was sound and the execution was disciplined. What was missing was any explanation of why. We followed instructions. There were before and after photographs and a celebration at the end of the week. Two months later the line looked like the before photographs.

That isn't a failure of effort or of the method. It's what happens when improvement is installed rather than understood, and it's why everything here is built to transfer capability instead of delivering a result. A tool that only works while someone is standing over it isn't a tool, it's a visit.

Later, managing an area that was struggling. It could hit uptime, or quality, or safety, but almost never together. The targets were fair and achievable, and never achieved. Time on the line watching the team made it clear that nothing needed adding. Things needed taking away. People were doing work because it had always been done, with nobody able to say why.

So some of it stopped, deliberately, to see what happened. What remained became a short list of non-negotiables and a standard for each role, built with the operators from the start. Everyone could cover every role, so team leaders could run a shift as long as each role had a person in it. The time freed up meant the non-negotiables got done, and when the non-negotiables got done, the targets came.

That sequence is the whole Opsteady Method in one area: watch before changing, stabilise before improving, improve from a base that holds.

And the other end of the job

Not every site is struggling. Some are good, and stuck, which is a harder problem because nothing is obviously broken and nobody can say what to aim at.

Once the low-hanging fruit is gone, it's about finding one percent, on repeat. Two habits did most of the work. The first was catching an initiative near its end, while the interest was still live and the data still valid, and asking the question that keeps a room thinking: great result, even better if. The second was yesterday's biggest problem, whatever it happened to be. Six hours of downtime in a week when demand needs every minute. Or the tools for routine maintenance not being in the station they belong in. Anything in between.

Taken one at a time, with discipline and no pressure attached to what must be delivered, that returns results steadily. It also changes what a team will tolerate. Once the standard is being met, challenge it, raise it a little, and repeat at a pace where people feel stretched rather than crushed. Done for long enough, teams stop waiting to be told, and stop waiting to be rescued.

That is also what eventually shows up on paper, in the form directors need to see.

The same principle held at a very different scale: making the case for over a million pounds of investment in new equipment and a reshaped shop floor. We went in with four options, covering different levels of investment, different impacts on people, and how the customer stayed protected throughout, and with one recommendation we were prepared to commit to. What carried the room wasn't the options. It was a value stream map built by the operations team themselves, and the hidden losses they had found in it that no spreadsheet would ever have surfaced. It was approved, and it paid itself back inside twelve months.

The people doing the work found the money. The case was won because they owned it.

One more thing, learned by watching

A supervisor sitting on a problem that wasn't even a large one, but which was blocking several other things from moving. He wasn't being lazy or difficult. Asked why he wasn't dealing with it, he said it plainly: look what happened to Joe when he raised something last time.

That's the day it became impossible to think of culture as a soft subject. Every tool in existence runs through the willingness of people to say what's actually happening. Where that willingness has been damaged, no system fixes it, and no amount of asking for openness in a meeting outweighs what people have already seen happen. It's why the Health Check reads behaviour alongside systems, and why nothing here pretends a template can substitute for trust.

What this means for what you're buying

Four plants, different sectors, different products, different problems, and the same pattern underneath every one of them. That's why this is sold as a method rather than a story. What travelled between those sites wasn't the solutions, because the solutions were different every time. It was the order they had to be done in.

Everything on this site has been used in a real operation, and most of it failed at least once before it worked. The failures are in the guidance, because they're worth more than the successes.

The aim is that you stop needing us. There's no retainer, no subscription to your own improvement, and no sales call following anything you download. If a tool does its job, you'll use it, adapt it, and eventually stop reading the instructions. That's the intended outcome, not a lost opportunity.

What we don't do

We don't come to your site. We don't run your projects. We don't tell you what your culture should be, because that's yours to shape and you know your people. And we won't claim to have invented ideas that have been documented for decades. Most of what's here builds on established thinking, credited where it's used. What Opsteady adds is deployment: what to do first, what conflicts with what, and what to do when it stalls.

Where to start

Supervisor Standard Work Pack

Every supervisor's day starts with the best intentions and ends wherever the loudest problem took them. There's nothing wrong with responding to a stopped line or a safety near miss, that's the job. The problem is what happens to everything else: the trainee two weeks into a critical task who was due a supervised check today, the quality hold that's been sitting since nights, the one person on shift who can run changeovers and is off sick tomorrow. None of it is urgent enough to interrupt whatever's already on fire, so none of it gets checked, and the site finds out about the gap only once it's become the next fire.

What it takes

Time
Roughly one full shift to shadow, a few hours to turn the log into a working sheet. Once running: 10 to 15 minutes a shift for the supervisor to walk the round itself, on top of the work they're already doing, not instead of it.
Who's involved
Owned by the supervisor the round is built for, agreed with whoever owns rollout across the rotation (a shift manager or team leader). The shadow shift itself should be run by that manager, not the supervisor watching themselves: people are reliably bad at seeing their own reactive patterns from the inside.
Materials
A Skills Matrix for this team makes the People column genuinely load-bearing, since it's where real single points of failure and in-progress transitions come from. Not a blocker to start, since the three critical-risk questions alone are enough to build a working round. A reason to revisit the People checkpoints specifically once a matrix exists, even a partial one. **The Dashboard's Round maturity calculation assumes a modern Excel or equivalent (Microsoft 365 or Excel 2021+)** — the underlying formulas use functions not available in Excel 2019 or earlier, which would show an error in that one cell rather than a value; everything else in the workbook works on any Excel version.

Common questions

FAQ

How is this different from a Layered Process Audit?

They sit at different levels. This module is one supervisor's own round through their own shift: what they personally check, in what order, every time they work. A layered process audit is a different mechanism entirely, higher levels of the organisation periodically checking whether standards, including this one, are actually being followed at the level below them. If you're asking "what does the supervisor check today," that's here. If you're asking "is the supervisor's own routine actually happening, verified by someone above them," that's Layered Process Audit System.

Do I need a finished Skills Matrix before I can build this?

No, but the People column is where a finished, or even partial, matrix earns its keep. Build the round on the three critical-risk questions in section 2 if a Skills Matrix doesn't exist yet for this team: safety, scrap or downstream failure, asset damage. Once a matrix exists, even a partial one covering the highest-risk tasks, come back and add anyone mid-transition on a level, and any single point of failure it's surfaced, straight into the People checkpoints. Building this without a matrix isn't wrong. Building it and never revisiting the People column once one exists is the mistake.

Won't a fixed round just turn into another form to fill in?

It can, and the test for whether it has is concrete, not a feeling. Look at the times and initials in the compliance register over two or three real shifts. Genuine walking produces natural variation: times that shift a little shift to shift, the odd gap where something legitimately ran long. A round filled from memory produces the opposite: identical timing patterns, everything completed in one block near the end of the shift, no gaps at all even on a genuinely bad day. The consequence of getting this wrong isn't cosmetic: a round being filled from memory has already stopped catching anything real, which means the whole reason it exists has quietly gone. See guide section 16.

What if my supervisors already have an informal version of this, a checklist someone made a few years back?

Don't throw it out, and don't skip the shadow shift either. What a supervisor believes they do from a checklist and what they actually do under real pressure are often two different things, which is exactly what a shadow shift catches that a review of the paperwork alone wouldn't. Check the existing version against the four categories and the real gaps from a Skills Matrix if one exists, and close what's missing rather than starting from nothing. See guide section 5, the minimum decision fork.

How many checkpoints is too many?

There's no fixed number, but a round with more than roughly ten to twelve checkpoints across all four categories for a single shift is usually starting to become something to complete rather than something to walk. If every stakeholder's request has earned a slot, that's the sign it's grown past the point of being usable. The fix is applying the three critical-risk questions and the exclusion rule in section 2 again, not adding more time to the round to fit everything in.

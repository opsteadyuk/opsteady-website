Data Analysis Basics

Most sites end up holding data they don't quite know how to read. A defect count nobody's ranked. A spec sheet with limits on it and no sense of whether the process can actually hit them reliably. A cluster of scrap that might be happening in the same spot every time, or might not, nobody's checked. And, sooner or later, two numbers about the same event that don't match: production's downtime log says 40 minutes, the shift report says an hour, and there's no way to settle it except an argument.

What it takes

Time
Pareto: none, it's covered fully inside Downtime Tracking. Cp/Cpk: about 30 minutes once you have 20 to 30 measurements already recorded. Hotspot mapping: an hour to set up the outline, then a few minutes a day to mark it. Reconciliation: as long as the actual disagreement takes to talk through, usually well under an hour once both sides bring their definitions rather than just their numbers.
Who's involved
Whoever already owns the relevant data. Reconciliation specifically needs both sides of the disagreement in the room, not one side relaying the other's position secondhand.

Common questions

FAQ

Which of the four tools do I actually need?

Depends on the question you're asking, not the data you happen to have. More named problems than time to fix them all: Pareto, inside Downtime Tracking & Pareto Pack. A spec exists and you're not sure the process can reliably hit it: process capability, Cp and Cpk. A defect might be tied to a specific location or step: hotspot mapping. Two numbers about the same event disagree: reconciliation, always first, before trusting either side. The Data Analysis Basics reference sheets carry the same four-line guide printed on its own for exactly this question.

What's the actual difference between Cp and Cpk, and why do I need both?

**Cp** measures whether the process's natural spread is narrow enough to fit inside the tolerance, without checking where inside the tolerance it's actually sitting. **Cpk** checks the same spread, but also accounts for how close the process is running to one edge of the spec rather than the other. The test: a process can have a strong Cp and a weak Cpk at the same time, narrow enough to fit the tolerance in principle, but running close enough to one edge that a small shift pushes it out of spec. Reading Cp alone would tell you the process is fine. Reading Cpk alongside it tells you it's fine today and fragile. Getting this distinction wrong means trusting a process that's actually one small drift away from failing.

Do I need a statistics background to use this?

No. Each tool is a short, specific method built to be usable without one. If a term is unfamiliar, standard deviation, spec limit, mean, the training document that comes with this module explains each from nothing, with no assumed knowledge.

Two of our systems disagree about the same thing. How do I know which one is right?

Usually neither is wrong. Section 5 of the guide walks through the reconciliation steps: state both numbers, write down exactly what each source counts and where its clock starts and stops, then compare the two definitions side by side rather than the two numbers. The gap almost always explains itself once both definitions are visible together. Picking whichever number is more convenient isn't reconciliation, and it guarantees the same disagreement resurfaces the next time it matters.

How many data points do I need before a Cp/Cpk number or a hotspot map actually means something?

For process capability, at least 20 to 30 recent measurements before calculating Cp or Cpk, fewer than that and the result isn't reliable enough to act on. For hotspot mapping, generally 10 to 15 occurrences before reading scattered marks as "no pattern," a handful of points scattered by chance looks identical to genuinely random defects, and you need enough of them to tell the difference.

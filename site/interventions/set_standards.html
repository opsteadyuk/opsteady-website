What it takes

Time
About 1 hour per process to capture it properly: watching the job, writing it, reading it back, correcting it until the operator confirms it. Setting up the register itself takes about 30 minutes. After that, holding it costs about 15 minutes per process every 6 months, plus whatever time a fix already takes, since updating the standard happens inside that time rather than alongside it.
Who's involved
The person who owns the register, one name, not a committee. The operator who does each job best, for the capture walk. Whoever signs off standards as correct at your site, who may be the same person as the owner or may not.
Materials
The Standards Register (Excel) and the SOP Template (Word), both supplied with this module. A notebook or tablet for the capture walk. Nothing else.

Common questions

FAQ

How do the three risk questions work with the override and the exclusion? Which one wins if they disagree?

They are not equal in weight, so there is no real conflict, only an order of operations. Run the three risk questions first: hurts someone, scraps the product or fails a downstream check, damages the asset. Any yes and it needs a standard, full stop. The override then adds processes the three questions might miss: if the job is done differently depending on who does it, and that changes the result, it needs a standard whether or not it scored as critical on the three questions. The exclusion only ever removes a process that scored no on all three questions and shows no variation by person. It cannot override a yes. If a process is safety-critical and also feels obvious, it still gets a standard. Obviousness only earns an exemption when there is genuinely nothing at stake and nothing to standardise.

What do we do when the written standard and what people actually do disagree?

The better of the two wins, decided on evidence, not on seniority and not on whichever version is older. In practice this means watching the job done, comparing it line by line against the document, and asking why each difference exists. Often the floor has quietly improved on the document over time: someone found a better sequence, a safer check, a faster set-up, and nobody told anyone or updated the paperwork. In that case, update the document. Occasionally the floor has drifted for a worse reason: a shortcut that happens to still produce acceptable output most of the time, or a habit nobody has questioned. In that case, the standard is right and the practice needs to change, with the reason explained, not just the correction issued. The test that tells you which is which: does the floor's version perform better, worse, or the same against the definition of done and the critical checks. If you cannot tell from evidence, that is itself useful information: it usually means the definition of done was never precise enough to judge by, which is worth fixing before anything else.

How does the register's "matches practice" column actually get checked, in practice?

Someone watches the job done and compares it against the current document, the same way the initial capture worked: watch, then check line by line. It takes about 15 minutes once a standard already exists, since you are confirming rather than writing from scratch. The result is one of three states: confirmed, with the date it was checked; not checked, meaning nobody has looked recently, which is an honest and useful answer, not a failure; or known gap, meaning someone has looked and found a specific difference that has not yet been resolved. The trigger points for a check are a scheduled confirmation walk, every 6 months by default, and anything that should prompt an unscheduled one: a process change, an incident, or a new starter reaching competence on that task. The one thing to avoid is marking something "confirmed" because a review is administratively due, rather than because someone actually watched the job. A confirmed date with nobody behind it is worse than an honest blank, because it hides exactly the gap the column exists to surface.

When should we use the Detailed version, the Standard version, or the Quick Guide?

Match the depth to who is reading it and why. Detailed is for training someone from zero, for regulated tasks that need the full reasoning on record, and as audit evidence. Standard is the everyday working version: refreshers, cross-training, day-to-day reference for someone who has done the job before but wants to check something. Quick Guide is for the point of use, one page, image-led, for someone who already knows the job and needs the key warnings and step order at a glance rather than the full explanation. Most sites and most standards only ever need the Standard version. Build a Detailed version specifically where regulation demands the extra evidence trail, or where a task is genuinely trained from a standing start often enough to justify it. Build a Quick Guide specifically where the Standard version is too long to use safely at the workstation. Whichever depths you build, build the Detailed or Standard version first as the single source of truth, and derive the others from it, never write them in parallel, since parallel versions drift apart from each other within a few revisions.

How does this relate to the Skills Matrix? Do we need both, and which first?

They are designed to reinforce each other, and the honest answer is that a skills matrix rating is not meaningful without a standard behind it. Rating someone as fully competent at a task with no written standard means rating them against an unwritten, unshared idea of what competent looks like, which is not something you can defend, teach consistently, or hand to a new assessor. Set Standards should come first, at least for the tasks you intend to rate people against. The two modules deliberately use the same three risk questions, so the list of tasks that need a standard and the list of tasks that belong on a skills matrix are, in practice, the same list. If a skills matrix already exists at your site and standards do not, start standards capture with exactly the tasks already on the matrix, in the order they appear there.

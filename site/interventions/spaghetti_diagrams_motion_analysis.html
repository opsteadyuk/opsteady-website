Spaghetti Diagrams / Motion Analysis

Unnecessary walking, reaching and material movement stop being something everyone feels and nobody has drawn.

£179Buy now
Overview

Most sites without this module make layout and method decisions from habit, from the original facility design, or from whoever last had a strong opinion in the room, rather than from what actually happens. Everyone on the floor can feel the waste — the operator who walks the length of the building twice a shift to fetch labels, the trolley route that loops back on itself for no clear reason — but feeling it and being able to point at it on paper are different things, and only one of them leads to a change that sticks.

This module teaches a simple observation and diagramming method: watch a person, a material, or a tool move through a real process, trace its actual path onto a floor plan, and use what the tangled result shows to shorten it. A path walked every day for years stops registering as a problem, the same way any sufficiently familiar waste does — drawing it, watching the line trace itself into loops and backtracks on the page, makes the invisible impossible to argue with. It's a practical observation and diagramming method, not facility redesign software or a simulation package, and it deliberately doesn't stop at diagnosis: it ends with at least one trialled layout or method change, a redrawn "after" path, and that improved path locked into the standard so it doesn't quietly drift back.

Done properly, the improved path is genuinely being used day to day, not just demonstrated once, and the team can show, concretely, the reduction in steps, distance or travel achieved.

At a glance
Time

A representative observation period — typically one full cycle or a fixed block of time long enough to capture normal variation, not a single idealised pass. Drawing the diagram itself takes minutes once observation is done.

Who's involved

Whoever will observe and trace the path, and, wherever practical, the person actually doing the walking or moving, since their own reaction to seeing the diagram is often what turns a data point into a genuine trial.

For your own site, decide: who chooses the area or process to diagram, ideally pointed there by a TIMWOODS walk or a value stream map that's already flagged motion or transport as a concern; who does the actual observing and tracing, someone who can watch a full representative period without changing how the work is normally done; who trials the layout or method change, named specifically, since a diagram with no owner for the next step tends to stay a diagram and nothing more; and who updates the standard and the workplace organisation once a change holds.

What you'll need

A simple floor plan or sketch of the area, accurate enough to place fixed points correctly. A pen, or a simple digital equivalent — no specialist software or tracking equipment is required. The working file, P7.9_Motion_Observation_and_Change_Log.xlsx, two tabs: a Motion observation tab (one baseline row per path, naming whether the person or the material/tool is being traced, distance per trip, trips in the period, and the biggest loop or backtrack seen, with a path traced from memory named as exactly that) and an After the change tab (one dated row per change, measured against the state the previous change left behind, so a path improved more than once is the method working, not an error).

Before you start

Choose one area or process sequence where motion or transportation waste is already suspected, rather than diagramming everywhere at once. Observe the actual path as it really happens, including the workarounds and shortcuts people have developed, not the idealised path a procedure document describes.

Work out first whether the waste you're chasing is primarily in how a person moves, or in how materials or tools move: a person's path is usually the right starting point when the suspected problem is walking distance or repeated reaching; a material or tool path is usually right when the suspected problem is transport distance or a confusing route between process steps; if both genuinely need tracing, draw them as two separate diagrams over the same period rather than combining them into one.

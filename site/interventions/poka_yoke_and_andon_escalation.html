Poka-Yoke & Andon Escalation

This module has two connected parts. The first is mistake-proofing: building the process itself so a known, recurring error becomes physically difficult or impossible to make, rather than relying on the operator to remember not to make it. The second is escalation: a clear, fast signal for the moment something abnormal happens anyway, so the right person finds out in minutes rather than at the end of the shift, or never.

What it takes

Time
A single poka-yoke and its paired Andon rule can usually be scoped, built and trialled inside one to two weeks for a genuinely simple, physical fix. Sensor-based or more involved mechanisms take longer.
Who's involved
The person who does the job, every time, without exception. A poka-yoke designed without them is a poka-yoke designed to be worked around. A supervisor or engineer to help translate the idea into something buildable. Whoever owns the area's Andon response, since they need to agree to the response time before it goes live, not discover it afterwards.
Materials
Whatever the specific mechanism needs, which varies. A locating pin or jig is a workshop job. A light-and-cord Andon signal is inexpensive and off-the-shelf. Keep the first build cheap and local; prove the mechanism before spending on anything more permanent.

Common questions

FAQ

Do we need special equipment or software to do this?

No, and starting from that assumption is the most common way this module gets stuck before it starts. Most effective poka-yoke and Andon systems on a real shop floor are physical or very simple digital: a locating pin, a jig, a counted-parts tray, a pull cord, a light. Save the sensor-based or software route for the specific case in section 11, where equipment already has it built in.

How do we choose which defect to fix first?

Pull the ranked list from the Non-Conformance Tracker (P3.1) or Customer Complaint log (P3.2) and take the top one, not the most recent or most memorable. This module is deliberately reactive to what the data already says is recurring, rather than a brainstorm of everything that could theoretically go wrong. See Pro section 5.

What's the actual difference between poka-yoke and Andon?

Poka-yoke stops the error happening in the first place. Andon is what tells the right person, fast, when something abnormal happens anyway. They are connected but distinct: a defect that can genuinely be built out of the process gets a poka-yoke, and a defect that depends on judgement a fixture cannot replicate gets a faster Andon response instead. See Pro section 18's first fork.

What counts as "responded" versus "resolved" on an Andon call?

Responded means someone has physically checked the actual cause at the workstation inside the agreed time. Resolved means the underlying issue has genuinely been fixed and a second person has verified it. Collapsing the two, so a light gets silenced from the office and logged as done, is one of the most common ways this module quietly stops working. See Pro section 16's fourth failure.

We inherited an Andon system from a parent company or a piece of purchased equipment. Do we need to build our own from scratch?

No. Rebuilding what already exists wastes effort and risks discarding something that works. The job instead is to audit whether the existing signal is genuinely acted on, whether any alarms have been quietly disabled because they trip too often, and whether the people using it actually trust it. See Pro section 11 and section 18's second fork.

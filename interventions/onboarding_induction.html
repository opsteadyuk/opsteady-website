Overview

Most sites don't have a bad onboarding process. They have no onboarding process — just whoever happens to be free that morning showing a new starter around and hoping the important things get mentioned, and "least busy" is rarely the same person as "best placed to induct someone." The gaps this leaves don't surface on day one: a safety point never covered, a standard nobody pointed them to, a first task shown once and never actually checked — these surface weeks or months later, usually at the worst possible moment, an incident, a quality escape, or a new starter who's quietly decided this site doesn't have its act together.

The fix is a day-by-day plan for the first fortnight, a named buddy chosen deliberately rather than by convenience, and a compliance sign-off checklist — all agreed in advance, not assembled on the morning someone starts. First tasks tie directly into Skills Matrix entry: a new starter is recorded at I level the moment their first supervised attempt happens, not retrospectively. The tell for whether it's actually being run day by day, rather than filled in retrospectively, is where the sign-off dates land across the fortnight — spread naturally across ten working days reads as real; a checklist that arrives fully signed on day 10 reads as a form being closed out.

This isn't a guess about what matters — a structured onboarding process measurably changes whether someone stays: new hires who go through one are around 58% more likely to still be with the organisation after three years, while close to a third of all new hires leave within six months for reasons that trace back to exactly the gaps this tool targets.

At a glance
Time

Half a day to build the generic week-one and week-two template, once. After that, the buddy's time is the real ongoing cost — fairly constant through week one, tapering through week two.

Who's involved

Whoever owns onboarding on the floor (usually a team leader or supervisor), to build and own the plan. A named buddy for every new starter, chosen for patience, proximity and genuine willingness rather than seniority — the most senior person available is rarely the best buddy if they don't have the time or temperament for it. Whoever owns the Skills Matrix and Set Standards, so the induction points at what's actually current.

For your own site, decide: who assigns the buddy and on what basis beyond availability; who signs off each checkpoint, and what happens if a sign-off is missing by its agreed date; and who gets told if a new starter clearly isn't tracking against the plan.

What you'll need

The working file (P1.3_Onboarding_Induction_Pack.xlsx). The current Skills Matrix, so first tasks and the I-level entry point are agreed in advance, not guessed at on day one. Your site's real, current standards, not a folder nobody's opened this year.

Before you start

Build the generic template before the next new starter arrives, not the morning they turn up — a plan assembled under pressure on day one is exactly the ad hoc pattern this tool exists to replace. Agree buddy selection criteria once, in writing, rather than defaulting to "whoever's free" each time. Tell the buddy they've been chosen, and roughly what it involves, before the new starter's first morning.

If some version of this already exists informally, audit it against four requirements rather than starting over: a day-by-day plan, a named buddy chosen deliberately, first tasks tied to Skills Matrix entry, and a compliance sign-off checklist pointed at current standards.

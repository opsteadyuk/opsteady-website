SMED / Changeover Reduction Toolkit

Changeover time cut without buying anything, by separating what genuinely needs the machine stopped from what never did.

£179Buy now
Overview

A changeover takes as long as it takes because nobody has ever separated what genuinely requires the machine to be stopped from what could happen while it's still running. Most sites treat changeover time as fixed, a fact of the equipment rather than a designed sequence — it rarely is. The method is to film or time one real changeover step by step, tag every step internal (machine must be stopped) or external (could happen before or after), convert as many internal steps to external as genuinely possible, streamline whatever's left, then re-time and write down the new sequence so it holds.

On a real stamping-line die changeover, timing the actual sequence rather than trusting the site's own 45-minute estimate found 52 minutes, all six steps treated as internal purely by habit. One SMED pass — pre-staging the die before the stop, quick-release clamps instead of an 8-bolt fixing, a pre-calibrated shim kit built externally — cut it to 20 minutes, with zero capital spend. Twenty minutes is real progress, not the finish line: the classic SMED target is single-digit minutes, and that typically takes several passes, not one.

Done properly, the reduction gets written into the standard and the pre-staging step gets built into standard work or the shift handover so it survives a shift change, not just the day it was filmed — and once it's held for a few cycles, the same method re-runs on the next highest-pain changeover, since a single successful pilot treated as finished is one of the most common ways this quietly stops working.

At a glance
Time

Half a day to film or time one real changeover properly, plus the same again to work through tagging and conversion with the people who actually run it.

Who's involved

Whoever actually performs the changeover, in the room, not just observed — they know which steps are genuinely fixed and which have always felt like dead time. A second pair of eyes, a supervisor or engineer, helps catch conversion options the person doing it every day has stopped noticing.

For your own site, decide: which asset genuinely has the highest changeover pain, most time lost, most frequent changeovers, or both; who's filming or timing, and who's actually doing the changeover being filmed; and who owns writing the new sequence down once it's proven, so it survives past the pilot.

What you'll need

A stopwatch or a phone camera — nothing else is required for the first pass, deliberately, since buying equipment before checking what converts or streamlines for free is one of the ways this tool gets used badly. Quick-release clamps or pre-set kits are a real second-pass tool, not a requirement to see the first result.

Before you start

Tell whoever schedules the asset, since a slower-than-usual changeover during filming is expected and shouldn't be treated as a problem on the day. Pick the highest-pain asset — the changeover costing the most capacity by frequency, duration, or both; if Bottleneck Analysis Tool is already in use and the highest-pain changeover sits on the confirmed bottleneck, start there, since it's exactly the free-first move that tool's own Exploit step calls for.

If a step genuinely can't convert to external — a mandatory cleaning cycle, a regulatory hold time, a documented sign-off — name it explicitly as a real floor the changeover can't shrink past, rather than treating a smaller-than-hoped reduction as a failed pilot.

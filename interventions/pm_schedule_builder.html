PM Schedule Builder & Compliance Tracker

PMs done on time, on the assets that actually need them, not the first thing sacrificed under pressure.

Overview

Most sites have a PM schedule somewhere — a binder, a spreadsheet, a plan in someone's head. What most don't have is a schedule that survives contact with a bad week: the moment production is under pressure, PM is the easiest thing to push. Nobody decides to skip it outright, it just quietly slips a day, then a week, until an asset that was "due" a service breaks down and gets logged as an unlucky, unplanned event. Without a visible due date and a compliance number someone actually looks at, a skipped PM has no cost attached to it until the breakdown happens.

This module makes PM due dates visible before they're missed, coloured by proximity, and turns compliance into a number tracked and reviewed like any other, calculated from real completion data rather than estimated — because completed-on-time is counted from the same rows as PMs due, compliance can never read above 100%, a number that used to be reachable by miscounting two separate tallies. It builds directly on Downtime Tracking & Pareto Pack (P5.1): the critical asset list comes from that Pareto, not a separate guess, and this is the branch a site takes once its top cause is dominated by preventable breakdowns rather than genuinely unplanned ones.

Done properly, the schedule is reviewed monthly against Downtime Tracking's own Pareto, so the two stay connected instead of drifting into separate numbers nobody reconciles — and preventable breakdowns on covered assets genuinely start dropping, not just the compliance percentage climbing on its own.

At a glance
Time

Half a day to build the initial schedule for the critical assets, once the asset list and Downtime Pareto data already exist.

Who's involved

Whoever owns Downtime Tracking's Pareto, since that's where the critical asset list comes from. Whoever actually does the PM work — maintenance technicians or operators — since frequency and task definitions need to be realistic, not aspirational. Whoever has authority to protect PM time when production pushes back.

For your own site, decide: who owns building and revisiting the schedule as assets or tasks change; who has authority to protect a scheduled PM window when production pushes back; and who reviews the compliance percentage, and how often.

What you'll need

An asset list, even a rough one. Downtime Tracking's own Pareto data showing which assets are actually driving preventable breakdowns, not a guess at which ones feel important. The working file, P5.2_PM_Schedule_Compliance_Tracker_Workbook.xlsx, five tabs: How to use, PM schedule (reusing Skills Matrix's certification-tab colour logic — OVERDUE, DUE SOON, ON TRACK), PM completion log, Compliance dashboard (with a Period key and built-in checks against blank rows, out-of-order periods, and duplicate periods), and a removable Example.

Before you start

Start with the assets Downtime Tracking's Pareto has already flagged, not the whole site — a schedule covering three assets that are actually tracked and protected beats one covering every asset on site with none of them genuinely honoured. Tell whoever schedules production, since PM time needs to be planned for, not squeezed in around it, and tell whoever owns Downtime Tracking, since this schedule is what should start showing up as fewer preventable breakdowns in that Pareto over time.

Check first whether a PM plan already exists, even informally: if so, start from whatever tasks and frequencies are already being followed and check them against Downtime Tracking's Pareto to confirm they're actually covering the right assets, rather than building fresh. Separately, work out whether each asset's interval is manufacturer-mandated (not adjustable by local judgement — treat any pressure to stretch it as a compliance risk) or site-set (can be revisited deliberately as real data accumulates).

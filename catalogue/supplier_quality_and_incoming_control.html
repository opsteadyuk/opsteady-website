Supplier Quality & Incoming Control

This module extends quality ownership to the incoming boundary, the point where material from outside the site first becomes the site's problem. It has two connected parts. The first is defining what "good" looks like on arrival for the items that actually matter, and choosing the lightest verification method that genuinely confirms it. The second is logging supplier-caused defects in the same way internal defects already get logged, and feeding the pattern back to the supplier rather than absorbing it silently, batch after batch.

Common questions

Do we need to inspect everything that comes through the gate?
No, and treating that as the goal is one of the fastest ways this module fails. The whole discipline is choosing a proportionate verification method, full inspection, a certificate, skip-lot sampling, or dock-to-stock, based on actual risk history, not applying the same heavy check to everything regardless of whether it's ever caused a problem. See Pro section 3 and section 9's first failure.
How do we decide which items actually need checking?
Start from what's actually caused a stop, rework, or a customer issue in recent months, not a theoretical review of your whole supplier base. That real history is the honest basis for the critical-to-quality list. See Pro section 4 and section 5.
What's the point of logging a supplier defect if we don't tell the supplier?
There isn't one, which is exactly why this is named as one of the module's core failure modes. A log that stays internal protects nobody and fixes nothing, since the only party who can actually change the supplier's process never hears about the pattern. See Pro section 9's second failure and section 16's second failure.
We already have PPAP or ISIR requirements with our suppliers. Do we need this as well?
Align the two rather than duplicating them. A formal part-approval requirement is usually a point-in-time certification at launch; this module's ongoing verification and defect-feedback loop is what covers the delivery after delivery after that. Where the formal documentation genuinely satisfies an ongoing check, don't build a second layer on top of it. See Pro section 11 and section 18's second fork.
What if the supplier says the defect isn't their fault?
Treat the logged pattern as the start of a joint investigation, not an accusation, and stay genuinely open to the root cause sitting on your own side, in handling or storage, rather than at the supplier. The data is there to find the real answer, not to win an argument. See Pro section 17.
